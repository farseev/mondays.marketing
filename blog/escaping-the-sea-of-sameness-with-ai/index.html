Escaping the sea of sameness when everyone uses AI

3 Aug 2026 · 7 min read · Marketing Mondays editorial team · FAQ

The sea of sameness is what happens when brands in a category converge on the same messages, visuals and trends. Generative AI accelerates it, because similar tools produce similar averages. The way out is to feed AI something competitors lack: specific, evidenced audience tensions and a narrative you are willing to hold even when trends move.

Scroll any category feed in Singapore for five minutes and you will see it. The same hooks, the same trending audio, the same polished but forgettable copy. This Marketing Mondays signal asks why, and what a brand can do about it.

Why does AI make brands sound the same?

Language models predict the most likely output given a prompt. Ask several of them for "a warm, modern, customer-first brand voice" and you will get remarkably similar results. When competitors use the same tools, trained on the same internet, with similar prompts, convergence is the default outcome.

Listening data shows marketers already feel this pressure. Brand differentiation in saturated markets is a recurring pain point, along with AI content that needs heavy human revision and a reliance on discounting instead of value. The second pattern, chasing trends out of fear of missing out, makes it worse.

Is trend-chasing the problem or the symptom?

The symptom. Brands chase trends when they lack a narrative strong enough to stand on. If you do not know what you stand for, the trend feels safer. The core insight from SOMIN's listening is that authenticity outlasts trends: narrative beats trend because narrative compounds and trends expire.

That does not mean ignoring trends. It means asking whether a trend expresses your narrative, or just borrows attention.

How do you differentiate when everyone has the same tools?

Start from a tension, not a tone

Tone of voice is easy to copy. An audience tension, a specific conflict your customers feel, is much harder to copy because competitors have to find it first. SOMIN, the AI audience-research platform behind Marketing Mondays, surfaces these tensions from real posts. The Fujifilm case study shows how a brand built content around what its audience actually cared about rather than category conventions.

Give AI your evidence, not just your adjectives

Instead of prompting for "friendly and bold", prompt with "our audience feels X, says Y in their own words, and distrusts Z". The output gets more specific immediately.

Hold a narrative across trends

Decide on one or two story lines your brand will own for a year. Test every trend against them.

Measure distinctiveness, not just performance

If your best-performing content could carry a competitor's logo without anyone noticing, it is not building your brand.

Checklist: are you in the sea of sameness?

  • Cover your logo. Could the post belong to a competitor?
  • Does your brief name a specific audience tension, with evidence?
  • Did you join the last three trends because they fitted your story, or because everyone did?
  • Is discounting doing the work that differentiation should?
  • Do your AI prompts contain audience evidence, or only brand adjectives?

A worked example

Take a hypothetical Singapore skincare brand. Its AI-assisted content talks about "glowing, healthy skin" like every competitor. Listening reveals a sharper tension: people in humid climates feel that most routines are designed for somewhere else, and they are tired of products that feel heavy by midday. That is a narrative: skincare made for the weather you actually live in. Every trend can be tested against it. The AI prompts change from adjectives to that tension, and the output stops sounding like everyone else.

Where does discovery fit in?

Sameness is not only a social problem. If AI search assistants and answer engines describe your category in generic terms, they will describe you generically too. Our sister agency SNMRush works on this, building answer-ready content from the questions audiences actually voice so brands show up distinctly in search and AI answers. We cover the same shift in our episode on AI answers.

How do you get buy-in for a narrative?

Narratives fail inside organisations more often than in the market. Someone senior wants to join a trend, a sales team wants a discount push, and the story bends. The best defence is to agree the narrative in writing, with the audience evidence that supports it, before the pressure arrives. Make it a one-page document: the tension, the proof, the story you will tell, and the trends you will deliberately skip. When the next shiny moment appears, the conversation becomes "does this fit what we agreed?" rather than a debate about taste. That single page has saved more brands from sameness than any tool, because it gives the team permission to say no with confidence.

Review that page every quarter against fresh listening. Tensions shift, and a narrative that ignores new evidence slowly becomes another kind of sameness. The goal is consistency of story, not stubbornness about detail, and the evidence tells you which is which.

Definitions

  • Sea of sameness: a category where brands converge on similar messaging and visuals.
  • Audience tension: a recurring conflict or frustration voiced by real people that a brand can address.
  • Distinctiveness: how recognisably your work belongs to your brand.

Frequently asked questions

What is the sea of sameness in marketing?

A situation where brands in a category converge on similar messages, visuals and trends so that their content becomes interchangeable and forgettable.

Does AI-generated content make brands less distinctive?

It can, because similar models and prompts produce similar averages. Feeding AI specific audience evidence and a clear narrative makes its output more distinctive.

Should brands stop following trends?

Not entirely. Join trends that express your narrative and skip those that only borrow attention. A narrative held over time builds more value than any single trend.

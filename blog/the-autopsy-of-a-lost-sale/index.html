The autopsy of a lost sale

28 Sep 2026 · 7 min read · Marketing Mondays editorial team · FAQ

An autopsy of a lost sale is a structured, blameless review of why a customer who was close to buying walked away. Done well, it traces the loss back to a specific audience signal the team missed, such as an unanswered objection or a competitor's better offer, and turns it into one change you make next week.

"The Autopsy of a Lost Sale" is one of the Marketing Mondays episodes people bring up most when they meet us in person. It struck a nerve because nearly every marketer has a lost deal they still think about, and most teams never examine it properly. They either blame sales, blame the price, or quietly move on. This article expands the episode into a method you can run with your own team.

Why do most teams never examine a lost sale?

Because it feels like a trial. Someone made the call, wrote the email, designed the landing page or set the price, and an honest review risks pointing at them. In a profession where so many people already wrestle with imposter syndrome and a fear of being found out, a lost sale feels like evidence against you personally.

That is exactly why the autopsy has to be blameless. The aim is not to find a culprit. It is to find the signal. When you remove the fear, people tell you what they actually saw, and what they saw is usually the most valuable data you have.

What should a lost-sale autopsy look at?

We use five layers, working from the customer outwards.

  1. The trigger. What made this person start looking in the first place? A life moment, a problem at work, a competitor's ad?
  2. The question they asked. What did they search, ask a colleague, or type into an AI assistant? Did your content answer it?
  3. The objection. What stopped them? Price, risk, timing, trust, a feature, a feeling?
  4. The alternative. Who or what did they choose instead, including doing nothing?
  5. The silence. What did your team never hear because nobody was listening in the right place?

The fifth layer is where the real lessons hide. Most lost sales are not lost at the final meeting. They are lost weeks earlier, in a conversation the brand was not part of.

Where do you find the evidence?

Internal data tells you what happened inside your funnel. It rarely tells you why. For the why, you need the outside view: what people in the same position are saying publicly about the problem, the category and your competitors.

This is where SOMIN, an AI audience-research platform and the founding partner of Marketing Mondays, does the heavy lifting behind the episode. It reads real posts and comments and surfaces the tensions, tones and objections that keep recurring. Instead of guessing why a buyer hesitated, you can check whether hundreds of similar buyers voice the same hesitation. The Mothercare Singapore case study is a useful example of a brand using that kind of evidence to understand what parents actually worry about before they buy.

A worked example

Imagine a B2B software company in Singapore loses a deal at the final stage. The salesperson's notes say "went with cheaper option". The easy conclusion is that price was the problem.

The autopsy asks different questions. What did buyers in this category say online in the months before? Suppose the listening shows a recurring tension: finance teams worry that marketing tools report impressive returns that do not show up in actual profit. Now the loss looks different. The buyer did not choose the cheaper tool because it was cheap. They chose it because it felt less risky to defend to their CFO. Your pitch talked about headline ROAS; their real fear was being blamed for a number that did not add up.

That is a fixable problem. You change the message from "higher returns" to "returns your finance team will believe". Our sister brand FMedia was built on exactly this tension, tracking true profit rather than flattering ROAS.

Checklist: running a 45-minute lost-sale autopsy

  • Pick one lost sale from the past 30 days. One, not a batch.
  • Invite marketing, sales and whoever owns the product. No more than five people.
  • State the rule out loud: we are looking for signals, not culprits.
  • Walk the five layers: trigger, question, objection, alternative, silence.
  • Bring outside evidence: what are similar buyers saying publicly?
  • Agree one change to ship within a week, with a named owner.
  • Note the signal you missed and where you will listen for it next time.

What are the common causes of death?

After running this format with many marketers in our community, a few patterns come up again and again.

The unanswered question

The buyer asked something specific, and the only content available was generic. Increasingly, that question is asked to an AI assistant, and if your brand is not in the answer, you were never in the running.

The wrong proof

The brand offered proof that mattered to the marketing team, such as awards or reach, not proof that mattered to the buyer's boss.

The invisible competitor

The real alternative was not a rival brand but doing nothing, or building it in-house. Nobody positioned against that.

The trust gap

The buyer felt the brand was talking at them, not listening. This is the most common and the hardest to see from inside.

Why this belongs in a community, not just a meeting room

Autopsies inside one company are limited by one company's blind spots. When marketers from different sectors compare their lost sales, patterns jump out that no single team would spot. That is part of why the Marketing Mondays community exists: peers who can say "we lost one exactly like that, here is what we found" without anyone feeling exposed.

Trust facts, not fear. A lost sale is not a verdict on you. It is a signal you have not decoded yet.

Frequently asked questions

What is a lost-sale autopsy?

A short, blameless review of one lost customer or deal that traces the loss to a specific missed audience signal and agrees one practical change to make within a week.

Who should attend a lost-sale autopsy?

Keep it small: marketing, sales and the product owner, ideally no more than five people, with a clear rule that the session looks for signals, not culprits.
